Your Year 8 class can say <em>I play football</em> without trouble. They can ask the question just as easily in German: <em>Spielst du Fußball?</em>

But when they write the English question, you may read this: <em>Play you football?</em> A few lines later comes <em>Does he plays?</em> You have explained do-support twice, and it still does not stay.

## The mistake comes from a rule they already know

These students are not careless. They apply a rule that works perfectly in German. German makes a question by swapping the verb and the subject, for any verb: <em>Du spielst</em> becomes <em>Spielst du?</em>

English does that swap only with <em>be</em>, modal verbs and helping verbs. Every other verb needs <em>do</em>. So <em>Play you?</em> is correct German word order in English words. For a student, the new rule is one extra step.

The same thing happens with the third person. In <em>Er spielt</em> the <em>-t</em> stays on the verb in the question: <em>Spielt er?</em> In English the <em>-s</em> moves to <em>does</em>, and the main verb loses it. Students keep it on both: <em>Does he plays?</em>

## The second tense they never needed

Standard German has no progressive form. <em>Ich spiele Fußball</em> can mean <em>I play football</em> or <em>I am playing football</em>. Some regions say <em>ich bin am Spielen</em>, but this form is mostly spoken, and Duden labels it colloquial.

So students write <em>Look, he plays football now</em>. The sentence is not wrong in German. They have no reason to hear what is wrong in English.

## Teach the step, not the rule

Start with <em>do</em> and nothing else. Put the German question and the English question on the board, word by word:

> Spielst du Fußball?<br>Do you play football?

Ask what <em>do</em> is doing. It is a marker that says "this is a question". Then show that it carries the <em>-s</em>:

> He plays football.<br>Does he play football?

Then give the second tense a question to answer, so it is not only a rule: is this every week, or right now?

- <em>Every Saturday, my brother ___ football.</em> (plays)
- <em>Be quiet. My brother ___ football on the phone.</em> (is playing)

Words such as <em>every Saturday</em>, <em>usually</em> and <em>never</em> point to the present simple. Words such as <em>now</em>, <em>at the moment</em> and <em>look!</em> point to the present continuous.

## A five-minute starter for your next lesson

Write five sentences on the board, each with one error of the types above:

- Play you football on Saturdays?
- Does she plays the guitar?
- Look! The dog sleeps on the sofa.
- What you do every morning?
- Mum cooks dinner at the moment.

Students work in pairs, correct the sentences, and say which German habit caused each mistake. Naming the cause helps them spot the same mistake in their own writing.
