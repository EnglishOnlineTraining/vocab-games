Before you book Business English training, check six things: who teaches, how the programme is designed, what material it uses, how you will see progress, what the contract commits you to, and what you can see before you pay. A provider who answers all six in writing is a safe choice. A provider who dodges two or more is not.

You have a budget, a team that writes and speaks English every day, and three offers that read almost the same. All three say “tailor-made”. None says what that means.

The checklist in one minute

  1. Trainer: a named person who will teach every session.
  2. Needs analysis: done before any proposal, not after.
  3. Material: your team’s real emails, calls and meetings, not a fixed course book.
  4. Progress: measured against written objectives, not attendance.
  5. Contract: session price, minimum commitment, cancellation terms and VAT, all stated.
  6. Proof: a free first conversation or sample before you decide.

Who will teach my team?

Ask for the name of the trainer, their qualifications and their business experience. Then ask whether that same person teaches every session. Some language schools sell the course and pass the group to a freelancer. A good answer names one person and says what happens if that person is ill.

How do you find out what my team needs?

A good provider starts with a needs analysis. That means a conversation with you, a level check for the participants, and a look at the English they handle at work. A weak answer is a course book chosen before anyone asks a question.

What material will the training use?

Training works best when participants practise on their own texts: the email they must send on Friday, the update they give every Monday. Ask to see a sample of the material. If every client gets the same book, the training is not tailored.

How will we know it worked?

Ask for objectives in writing before the first session. Make them observable. “Write a client email in ten minutes without asking a colleague to check it” is an objective. “Improve fluency” is not. Ask how the provider will report progress against these objectives. Attendance lists do not show whether anyone improved.

A CEFR level (A1 to C2) is a useful starting point for grouping people. It is not an objective by itself.

What does the contract commit me to?

Ask for the price per session, the minimum number of sessions, the cancellation rules and whether VAT is included. Ask what happens when participants miss a session. Ask whether sessions are online or on site, and how large the group will be. If the answer is vague, expect the invoice to be vague too.

What can I see before I decide?

A reliable provider lets you judge the quality first: a free first conversation, a written proposal, or sample exercises. You should never have to sign before you know what the training looks like.
