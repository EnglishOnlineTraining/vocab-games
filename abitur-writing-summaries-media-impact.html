📘 Referenzleitfaden: Zusammenfassungen schreiben (zum Nachschlagen)
Present tense — summaries of written texts are written in the simple present, even if the article uses the past.
Example: "The article explains that..." (not "explained")
Reporting verbs — use verbs like states, argues, suggests, claims, points out instead of "says".
Example: "The writer argues that ambition has changed shape."
Objectivity — summaries report the writer's ideas, not your own opinion.
Example: Avoid "I think..." or "In my opinion..."
Umbrella sentences — group several specific details under one general statement.
Example: Instead of listing three examples, write: "The article gives several examples of this trend."
Conciseness — cut examples, repetition, and rhetorical flourishes; keep only the core content.

The Feed That Never Sleeps: How Social Media Rewired the American Mind

Adapted from a US media-affairs feature

Twenty years ago, the average American got their news from a handful of evening broadcasts and a folded newspaper. Where do you get your news now — and can you honestly say you remember the last time you checked only once a day? The answer, for most people, is no.

Take Jordan Mills, a 19-year-old college student in Atlanta. He checks his phone within ninety seconds of waking up, scrolls through six different news apps before breakfast, and estimates he sees over 200 headlines before his first class even begins. He is, by his own admission, exhausted by information he never asked for.

He is not unusual. A 2023 Pew Research study found that 86% of American adults now get at least some of their news from a smartphone, and nearly half report feeling "worn out" by the sheer volume of news they encounter. The feed, it seems, has become both a lifeline and a burden.

Media companies insist they are simply meeting demand. Critics counter that they have manufactured it. The algorithm does not care whether a headline informs you or merely inflames you — it only cares whether you keep scrolling. Attention, once freely given, is now the most valuable currency in America.

Whatever side of that argument one takes, one thing is undeniable. The feed never sleeps, and increasingly, neither do we.

Selbsteinschätzung

Model summary:

The article examines how constant access to news through smartphones has changed media consumption habits in the USA. It uses the example of a college student who checks multiple news apps within minutes of waking up to illustrate how normalised near-constant news consumption has become. The writer cites survey data showing that the majority of American adults now get news from smartphones, and that many feel overwhelmed by its volume. The article also highlights a debate over responsibility: media companies claim they are meeting audience demand, while critics argue that algorithms are designed to maximise attention rather than inform. It concludes that this constant news cycle has become a defining, and demanding, feature of modern life.
