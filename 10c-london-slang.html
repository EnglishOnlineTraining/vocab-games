Is London Slang Dead?

For centuries, London has been the birthplace of bold new language. From Cockney rhyming slang to modern "street speak," the capital's young people have always invented creative ways to talk. But is this tradition dying?

A recent study suggests that traditional Cockney slang is less common among London teenagers today. Words like "apples and pears" (stairs) and "dog and bone" (phone) were once understood by everyone in East London. Now, even young Londoners don't always know them. Meanwhile, new slang emerges online — "safe," "fam," "wet" — borrowed from music, social media, and youth culture across the UK and beyond.

Some educators worry that slang excludes people and complicates learning. Others argue that slang is how young people express identity and belong to a group. They say that dismissing slang as "bad English" ignores how language naturally evolves. After all, today's slang becomes tomorrow's standard English.

"Young people aren't abandoning London culture," says linguist Dr. James Chen. "They're redefining it. Slang always dies and is reborn. That's how languages stay alive."

Discussion: Language, Culture & Change

Share your views on how language evolves and what it means for society.

Prompt

The article suggests that young people are redefining London culture through new slang, rather than abandoning it. Do you think this is a positive thing for English language and culture? Why or why not?

Write 100–150 words. Consider:

  • How does slang help young people express identity?
  • Are there risks when groups use language that others don't understand?
  • Is language change natural and healthy, or a problem to solve?
