Reading: Scotland’s Fight for Freedom

Scotland’s Fight for Freedom

For centuries, Scotland was organised around the clan system. The word “clan” comes from the Gaelic clann, meaning “children” or “descendants.” Each clan was led by a chief who provided protection, settled disputes and controlled the land. In return, clan members owed their chief loyalty and military service. Although many members shared blood ties with the chief, outsiders could also join a clan simply by swearing allegiance. The clan system was strongest in the Highlands and survived for hundreds of years before it was eventually destroyed after the Battle of Culloden in 1746.

Scotland’s struggle for independence began in 1286, when King Alexander III died without a clear heir. Edward I of England was invited to help choose the next king, but he used the opportunity to claim authority over Scotland instead. When the Scots resisted, Edward invaded in 1296, seizing the Stone of Destiny — the ancient coronation stone — and taking it to London as a symbol of English dominance.

The first great hero of Scottish resistance was William Wallace. In 1297, Wallace led a smaller Scottish force to a stunning victory over the English at the Battle of Stirling Bridge, using the narrow bridge to neutralise the English advantage in numbers and cavalry. He was appointed Guardian of Scotland, but his success was short-lived. At the Battle of Falkirk in 1298, Edward I deployed Welsh longbowmen to devastating effect, and the Scottish army was crushed. Wallace spent years as a fugitive before he was captured in 1305 and executed in London.

After Wallace’s death, Robert the Bruce took up the cause. He had himself crowned King of Scots in 1306 and fought a long guerrilla campaign against the English. His greatest triumph came at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314, where his outnumbered forces decisively defeated Edward II’s army. However, England still refused to recognise Scotland’s independence.

In 1320, Scottish nobles sent the Declaration of Arbroath to Pope John XXII. This remarkable document argued that Scotland had always been a free and independent kingdom, and that the Scots had the right to choose their own king. Many historians consider it one of the earliest statements of national self-determination. As a result of diplomatic pressure, England finally recognised Scotland as independent in the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton in 1328.

Ironically, Scotland later voluntarily joined with England. In 1707, the Act of Union merged the two parliaments into Great Britain. Many Scots opposed the union, and the debate has continued ever since. In 2014, Scotland held a referendum on independence, and 55% voted to remain part of the United Kingdom. The question of Scottish independence, however, remains very much alive in modern politics.
