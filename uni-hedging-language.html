Reading & Hedging Analysis

Read the academic text below. The highlighted words and phrases are examples of hedging language. Then answer the questions that follow.

What is hedging? In academic writing, hedging means using cautious language to avoid making claims that are too strong or absolute. Writers hedge when they are uncertain, when evidence is limited, or when they want to leave room for other interpretations. Hedging is not a sign of weakness — it shows intellectual honesty.
Urbanisation and Mental Health

Over the past two decades, a growing body of research has suggested that rapid urbanisation may have significant effects on the mental health of city residents. While cities tend to offer better access to healthcare and employment opportunities, some researchers argue that the pace of urban life could contribute to higher levels of stress and anxiety.

A 2023 meta-analysis of 45 studies indicated that people living in densely populated urban areas were approximately 20% more likely to report symptoms of depression than those in rural communities. However, it is important to note that this correlation does not necessarily imply causation. Other factors, such as income inequality, social isolation, and access to green spaces, appear to play a substantial role.

It is possible that urban planning strategies which prioritise green infrastructure and community spaces might help to reduce these negative effects. Preliminary evidence from cities such as Copenhagen and Singapore suggests that well-designed public spaces can potentially improve residents’ psychological well-being, although further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Exercise C

Hedging Transformation

The sentences below are too strong or absolute for academic writing. Rewrite each one using hedging language to make it more appropriate. Use the word or phrase given in brackets.

1
“Social media causes depression in teenagers.”   [may / contribute to]
Tip: Avoid stating a direct cause. Instead, suggest a possible connection.
2
“All students learn better with technology in the classroom.”   [some / tend to]
Tip: Avoid generalising about “all” students. Limit the scope.
3
“This study proves that a plant-based diet is healthier than a meat-based diet.”   [suggests / could be]
Tip: A single study rarely “proves” anything. Use a softer reporting verb.
4
“Online learning is always more effective than face-to-face teaching.”   [in some contexts / appears to be]
Tip: Replace “always” with a limiting phrase and soften the comparison.
5
“Immigration is the only solution to Europe’s ageing population problem.”   [one possible / might help to]
Tip: Avoid presenting a single solution as the only option.
