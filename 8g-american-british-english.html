Exercise C

Register: Informal → Formal

Rewrite each informal sentence in a more formal style. Avoid contractions and slang. Use full forms and polite language.

Tip box

Informal → Formal changes:
• Contractions → full forms (I'm → I am)
• Phrasal verbs → single verbs (find out → discover)
• Slang/casual words → neutral words (kids → children)
• Short sentences → longer, connected sentences

Exercise D

Reading: English Around the World

Read the text and fill in the gaps with the correct word.

Text

English is spoken in many countries around the world, but it is not always the same everywhere. The two main varieties are American English and British English. Although speakers of both varieties can understand each other without much difficulty, there are some interesting differences in vocabulary, spelling and pronunciation.

One major area of difference is vocabulary. For example, what Americans call a , the British call a pavement. When an American goes on , a British person goes on a holiday. These vocabulary differences developed because the two countries were separated by the Atlantic Ocean for hundreds of years.

Spelling is another area where the two varieties differ. The British prefer to write while Americans write color. Similarly, British people write centre, but Americans spell it . Many of these spelling differences go back to Noah Webster, who published an American dictionary in 1828 and simplified many British spellings.

Register — how formal or informal your language is — also plays a role. In formal writing, you should avoid like don't or can't and use the full forms instead. In informal language, people often use like find out instead of the more formal discover.
