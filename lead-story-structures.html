5 Story Structures for English Writing

Five simple shapes that turn a list of facts into a text people read to the end — for emails, reports, case studies and interviews.

How to use it: pick the structure that fits your text, copy the template line, and fill in your own details. Each card links to a full guide with more examples.

1 · And-But-Therefore

Popularised by scientist and filmmaker Randy Olson

Use it for: Any short text that feels like a flat list of facts.

[Situation] and [what was normal]. But [the problem]. Therefore, [what happened next].

“We launched a new feature, and the first week went well. But by week three, almost nobody used it. Therefore, we called ten users and rebuilt it.”

2 · Hero's Journey (short version)

Joseph Campbell (1949); 12 stages by Christopher Vogler

Use it for: “Tell me about yourself” in an interview, an “About me” page.

Where I started → what changed → who helped me → my hardest problem and how I solved it → what I can give you now.

“… The hardest moment came last winter, when a supplier failed two days before a big delivery. I found a new partner and we delivered on time. Now I want to bring that experience to your team.”

Read the full guide →

3 · Before-After-Bridge

A classic copywriting formula with no single originator

Use it for: Emails that propose a change, reports that show results.

Before: the problem now. After: the result. Bridge: how you got from one to the other.

“Response time was 48 hours. Response time is now four hours. We built a help centre that answers 60 per cent of questions first.”

Read the full guide →

4 · Three-Act Structure

Aristotle, Poetics; modern model by Syd Field, Screenplay (1979)

Use it for: Case studies and “how we solved it” reports.

Act 1: the client and what is at stake. Act 2: the false starts and hard decisions — the longest act. Act 3: the result in numbers and one lesson.

“We rewrote the emails. Nothing changed.” — two honest sentences in Act 2 build more trust than a page of adjectives.

Read the full guide →

5 · Challenge-Choice-Outcome

Public narrative, taught by Marshall Ganz at Harvard Kennedy School

Use it for: Writing about a hard decision: interviews, reviews, project reports.

Challenge: a problem where every option looks bad. Choice: “We considered … but decided … because …”. Outcome: the honest result and one lesson.

“We lost 18 per cent of the pipeline. But our close rate doubled. The lesson: the obvious trade-off is rarely the only one.”
