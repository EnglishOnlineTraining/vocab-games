📘 Reference Guide: Rhetorical Devices
Anaphora — repetition of the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses or sentences.
Example: "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields."
Metaphor — describing something as if it were something else, without using "like" or "as".
Example: "The city was a jungle of concrete and noise."
Allusion — an indirect reference to a well-known person, event or text that the audience is expected to recognise.
Example: "She called her start-up her 'moonshot', echoing Kennedy's promise to put a man on the moon."
Tricolon (list of three) — three parallel words or phrases for rhythm and emphasis.
Example: "…government of the people, by the people, for the people."
Antithesis / contrast — placing two opposing ideas close together for effect.
Example: "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."
Rhetorical question — a question asked to make a point rather than to get an answer.
Example: "Who among us has never made a mistake?"
Statistic — a number or piece of data used as evidence.
Example: "Almost two thirds of the world's population now owns a mobile phone."

The Rainbow Is Not a Finish Line: Thirty Years of South African Democracy

Adapted from a keynote address at the Born-Free Youth Summit, Johannesburg

Thirty years ago, on an autumn morning much like this one, millions of South Africans stood in queues that curved around schoolyards, church halls and dusty township fields, waiting to cast the first free vote of their lives. Those queues became the first threads of a new national fabric, woven from patience, from courage, and from a hope that had survived decades of brutality. Some of your grandparents stood in those lines for six hours; when they finally emerged, inked fingers held high, they wept — not because the struggle was over, but because it had finally begun to count.

You are the generation they queued for. You are the children of "Madiba magic" — the born-frees, raised in the long shadow of Nelson Mandela's walk to freedom. You did not choose this inheritance, but it is yours: a constitution admired across the world, and a country still learning to live up to it.

Let no one tell you that nothing has changed. We have built schools where once there were only trees for shade. We have brought clean water to villages that carried it on their heads for generations. We have opened university gates to young people whose parents were turned away from them. That is not nothing. That is the ground on which you stand.

And yet honesty is the rent we pay for living in a free country. Almost half of young South Africans who want to work cannot find a job. The child in rural Limpopo and the child in a Sandton suburb still do not share the same classroom, the same hospital queue, or the same chance. What is freedom, if it does not put food on the table? What is a vote, if it cannot buy a textbook?

The promise of 1994 was never a promise of miracles. It was the promise of a country that works — a country that works for the nurse in Soweto, for the farmer in the Karoo, and for the student sitting in this hall. That promise is not yet kept. But promises of this size are not kept in a single generation; they are handed on, like a baton in a relay race that no one runner can finish alone.

So do not let this anniversary become a museum. Memory is a compass, not an anchor. The generation of 1976 faced the dogs and the tear gas so that you could face something harder: the slow, unglamorous work of building — passing exams, starting businesses, holding officials to account, voting in every election, not just the loud ones.

My generation's task was to break the chains. Yours is to build the house. The next chapter of this country will not be written in the Union Buildings; it will be written by you — in your lecture halls, in your workshops, in your community meetings, and on your ballots. Take the compass. Run your leg of the race. The rainbow was never the finish line; it is where the real work begins. Thank you.

Self-Assessment

Model paragraph:

The speaker anchors her appeal in a mounting anaphora — "We have built schools… We have brought clean water… We have opened university gates" — whose accumulated rhythm transforms national progress into a shared, collective achievement in which every listener can take pride. She then invokes an allusion to "Madiba magic" and Mandela's "walk to freedom", borrowing the moral authority of the country's most revered figure to present the born-free generation as the rightful heirs of his struggle. Finally, the speech closes with a direct address — "it will be written by you" — which deliberately shifts responsibility from the speaker's generation to the audience, converting reflection into a personal call to action. Together, these devices allow the speaker to balance pride with urgency: her listeners are made to feel both grateful heirs and necessary agents of an unfinished project.
