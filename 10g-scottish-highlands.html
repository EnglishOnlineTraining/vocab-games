The Scottish Highlands: Between Reality and Legend

The Scottish Highlands stretch across the northern half of Scotland, covering more than 25,000 square kilometres of rugged mountains, deep glens and dark lochs. It is one of the most sparsely populated regions in Europe — in some areas, the population density is as low as 1.6 people per square kilometre, which is even lower than in Lapland. Despite this remoteness, or perhaps because of it, the Highlands have produced some of the richest folklore traditions in the British Isles.

Life in the Highlands has never been easy. For centuries, communities survived through sheep farming, fishing and small-scale agriculture on land that was often poor and exposed to harsh Atlantic weather. The infamous Highland Clearances of the 18th and 19th centuries displaced thousands of families from their ancestral lands, as wealthy landowners converted vast areas into sheep farms and deer-stalking estates. The effects of those clearances are still visible today: abandoned stone cottages stand in silent valleys, and many communities continue to struggle with depopulation as young people leave for the cities in search of better opportunities.

Yet the Highlands remain deeply fascinating, not least because of their extraordinary mythology. The most famous creature is, of course, the Loch Ness Monster. The first recorded sighting dates back to 565 AD, when the Irish monk Saint Columba reportedly encountered a “water beast” in the River Ness. Since then, thousands of people have claimed to see something strange in the dark waters of Loch Ness, and the mystery continues to attract over half a million visitors each year.

Less well known internationally, but deeply rooted in Highland culture, are the kelpies — shape-shifting water spirits said to haunt Scotland’s lochs and rivers. According to legend, a kelpie typically appears as a beautiful grey or white horse standing at the water’s edge. Travellers who climb onto its back find themselves unable to dismount as the creature drags them beneath the surface. One identifying feature, so the stories say, is that its hooves are reversed compared to a normal horse. The kelpie legends served a practical purpose: they warned children to stay away from dangerous waterways. Today, the myth is celebrated in Andy Scott’s enormous steel sculptures near Falkirk, known simply as “The Kelpies” — two thirty-metre-high horse heads that have become one of Scotland’s most recognisable modern landmarks.

Another beloved figure in Highland folklore is the selkie — a creature that is part seal and part human. Selkies live as seals in the sea but can shed their skin to walk on land in human form. Unlike kelpies, selkies are gentle and melancholic rather than dangerous. Many selkie stories tell of fishermen who fall in love with a selkie woman after finding her seal skin on the shore. If the fisherman hides the skin, the selkie must remain human and live with him — but she always longs for the sea. These tales, which originated in Orkney and Shetland, explore themes of freedom, belonging and the tension between two worlds.

The Highlands today are caught between preservation and change. Tourism brings vital income but also puts pressure on fragile communities and landscapes. Housing shortages make it difficult for young families to stay. And while the ancient stories of kelpies and selkies may no longer frighten people away from the water, they remain a powerful reminder of how closely Highland culture has always been connected to its wild, untamed landscape.

Past Tenses in Narrative Writing

When telling a story or writing a diary entry, we use different past tenses for different purposes. Choose the correct form for each gap in this Highland story.

Remember the three narrative tenses: Simple past — completed actions: She walked to the loch. Past continuous — background / ongoing actions: The wind was howling across the moor. Past perfect — earlier events (before the main story): She had never seen such a wild place.

Exercise D

Writing: A Diary Entry from the Highlands

Imagine you are spending a week in the Scottish Highlands. Write a diary entry (120–160 words) about one day of your trip.

Guidelines

Your diary entry should describe what happened during one day in the Highlands. You could write about:

• A walk through a remote glen or along a loch

• Something strange or unexpected you saw or heard

• A conversation with a local person about Highland life or folklore

• Your thoughts and feelings about the landscape and atmosphere

Try to include:

• All three past tenses (simple past, past continuous, past perfect)

• At least three vocabulary items from Exercise C (e.g. rugged, folklore, melancholic)

• Descriptive language to bring the scene to life
